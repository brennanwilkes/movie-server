Written 2026-08-01, on top of the 2026-07-31 data-collection pass plus a day of
empirical playback testing on the live Fire Stick and a re-pull of the library.

Library at time of writing: **860 movies + 1,696 episodes = 2,556 files, 5.04 TB**
(2.45 TB movies, 2.58 TB TV) on a 7.3 TB drive with **2.5 TB free**.

## 1. The bottleneck is not where the software thinks it is

Ranked by how much each link actually constrains what you see and hear:

| Rank | Link | Constraint | Currently modelled in code? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Projector** — WiMiUS K5, **native 1280x720**, 480 ANSI lumens | Discards half the pixels of every 1080p file and crushes shadow detail | **No. Not modelled anywhere.** |
| 2 | **Speakers** — PreSonus Eris 3.5, **stereo pair** | Every audio format collapses to 2.0 | **No. And the UI rewards the opposite.** |
| 3 | **Fire Stick** — MT8127, no HEVC Main10 | 544 files force a server transcode | Yes, correctly |
| 4 | **NUC** — no Main10 hardware decode | Those transcodes run at 1.29–2.1× realtime | Yes, correctly |
| 5 | **Disk** — 2.5 TB free | Real but not urgent | Partly |
| 6 | **Network** — gigabit wired both ends | Not a constraint at any bitrate we hold | Correctly ignored |

Your instinct in the prompt was right, and stronger than you put it. You asked
whether you're "optimizing for the NUC but really the firestick and projector are
crushing everything anyway." **The projector is crushing everything.** It is the
only component in the chain that degrades every single file unconditionally, and
it is the one component that appears nowhere in the codebase.

## 2. Only one thing in the whole library makes the server work

Tested by driving the real Fire Stick session and reading Jellyfin's settled
decision (method and full results: `raw/playback-tests-2026-08-01.md`):

- 19.7 Mbps H.264 → **DirectPlay**
- DTS 5.1, sole audio track → **DirectPlay** (client decodes it in software)
- TrueHD 7.1, sole audio track → **DirectPlay**
- FLAC → **DirectPlay**
- HEVC **8-bit** 1080p → **DirectPlay**
- HEVC **Main10** → **TRANSCODE**, `VideoProfileNotSupported`

`hevc-profile=main` in the client's device profile is the single dividing line in
this library. 544 files (21%, 0.53 TB) are on the wrong side of it — 524 of them
TV episodes.

Two corrections to the 2026-07-31 conclusions fall out of this:

- **The "326 files with no AC3/AAC fallback force an audio transcode" finding was
  wrong for the main client.** It direct-plays them. That concern is real only for
  the PS4, which you use 1% of the time.
- **HEVC 10-bit transcode is worse than estimated.** Not 3.32× realtime — measured
  at true 1080p, **2.1× idle and 1.29× with downloads running**, with 40 dropped
  frames client-side and server load going 4.1 → 14.3 from that one transcode.
  1.29× is not a comfortable margin. This is your stutter mechanism.

## 3. The bitrate colour bands are broken as a signal

`controller/web/js/util.js:16` — `≥15 purple, 8–15 green, 4–8 orange, <4 red`.

Measured against the library those bands now describe:

| Band | Movies | Episodes | Top 100 |
|---|---|---|---|
| purple ≥15 | 1.0% | 3.2% | 7.1% |
| green 8–15 | 4.4% | 19.3% | 25.0% |
| orange 4–8 | 11.9% | 16.7% | 19.6% |
| **red <4** | **82.7%** | **60.7%** | **48.2%** |

Movie bitrate percentiles: p10 1.43, **median 2.47**, p75 3.09, p90 6.15, max 19.70.

**83% of your movies render red.** A scale where five out of six items are in the
worst band is not conveying quality — it is conveying "this library is bad", which
is the one message you can't act on. You said you've been using purple/green as
the target for films you care about; on the current scale that target is
unreachable for most of the collection, because the collection simply isn't
encoded at those rates.

The deeper problem is that the bands are absolute Mbps, and Mbps is not comparable
across resolution, codec, grain, or runtime. `audit.js` already knows this — it
scales HEVC by ×1.8 (`X265_EFFICIENCY`) and has a content-aware floor
(`minRatioFor()`: 0.80 for documentaries, 0.70 for pre-1990 film, 0.30 otherwise).
The Library tab's badge knows none of it. **The two surfaces disagree about what
"good" means**, which is exactly what the shared-`bitrateCls` comment was written
to prevent.

## 4. Your source-tier intuition is right; the *labels* are lying

`release-rules.js:20` ranks Remux 5 > Bluray 4 = WEBDL 4 > WEBRip 3 > HDTV 2 > DVD 1.
That hierarchy is sound and matches the external consensus.

But the labels attached to your actual files do not track measured bitrate:

| *arr quality label | n | median Mbps | p10 | p90 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bluray-1080p | 716 | **2.64** | 1.97 | 6.34 |
| WEBDL-1080p | 19 | 4.28 | 1.93 | 11.77 |
| WEBRip-1080p | 19 | 2.64 | 1.97 | 3.92 |
| **Remux-1080p** | **3** | **3.09** | 2.18 | 4.54 |
| Bluray-720p | 68 | **1.00** | 0.89 | 1.39 |

A real 1080p remux is 20–35 Mbps. Three files claim `Remux-1080p` at ~3 Mbps —
that label is simply false. And `Bluray-1080p` at a 2.64 Mbps median is not
"Blu-ray quality", it's a heavy re-encode *of* a Blu-ray.

The reason is visible in the release groups:

| Group | n | median Mbps | total |
|---|---|---|---|
| YIFY | 151 | 1.94 | 226 GB |
| YTS.MX | 111 | 2.99 | 276 GB |
| YTS | 55 | 2.28 | 117 GB |
| YTS.AG | 27 | 2.17 | 51 GB |
| YTS.LT | 11 | 2.24 | 23 GB |
| **YTS family total** | **355 (41% of movies)** | **~2.2** | **~693 GB** |
| OFT | 36 | 6.65 | 192 GB |

**41% of your movie library is YTS/YIFY.** They are the reason the median is
2.4 Mbps. They are also, notably, all H.264 8-bit — so they direct-play perfectly.
They are cheap and safe and small; they are not "Blu-ray 1080p" in any meaningful
sense, whatever Radarr's label says.

## 5. HEVC is not paying for itself here

Movies: h264 median 2.38 Mbps (n=794) vs hevc median 2.53 Mbps (n=64). The HEVC
files are *larger* at the median. You are taking the 10-bit transcode risk without
collecting the compression dividend, because the releases being grabbed aren't
encoded to exploit it.

HEVC **8-bit** remains the genuinely best format for this hardware — smaller, and
both ends hardware-decode it. But it's rare in the wild, because 10-bit is the x265
scene default even for SDR.

## ⭐ The single highest-value finding: the mechanism exists and isn't switched on

Radarr has three quality profiles. `Beloved (best quality)` scores exactly the way
a film you care about should be scored — **+100 for a 6–10 GB file, +30 for 10–15 GB,
−500 for anything under 1.5 GB** — the inverse of `Normal`, which pays +80 for
1.5–3 GB and −150/−500/−1500 as files get bigger.

It demonstrably works:

| Profile | movies | median ratio vs target | grade mix |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Beloved (best quality)** | 36 | **1.01×** | 18 excellent · 11 good · 4 adequate · 3 compromised |
| Normal | 734 | 0.44× | 46 exc · 61 good · 231 adeq · 357 comp · 39 poor |
| Low (save space) | 90 | 0.47× | 5 exc · 9 good · 40 adeq · 34 comp · 2 poor |

Films on the Beloved profile land at **2.3× the quality ratio** of films on Normal,
and 80% of them are `good` or better.

**But only 34 of your 112 Top 100 films are on it.** 77 are on `Normal`, and *Meet
the Parents* is on `Low (save space)`. **70 of those 78 are below `good`.**

That is why Gladiator is 1.7 GB and Inception is 2.0 GB. It was never a rules
problem or a hardware problem — the right profile exists, it just isn't assigned.

Worklist written to **`WORKLIST-top100-profiles.txt`** — all 78 films with current
size, target size, current profile, quality label and release group.

**Changing a profile downloads nothing.** `upgradeAllowed=False` on all three
profiles (the deliberate no-auto-upgrade safety invariant), so reassignment is
inert until you deliberately search. It is the cheapest possible first step:
308 GB → 760 GB *if and when* you choose to act on all of them, and you can do it
film by film through the Audit tab.
